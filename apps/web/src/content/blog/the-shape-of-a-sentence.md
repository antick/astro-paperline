_This sample is a small type specimen, written to show how different voices sit on the page._

## Begin with a clear thought

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog. That familiar sentence brings every letter of the English alphabet into view. A less dutiful fox might stop by the river, admire the reeds, and forget why it was in such a hurry.

Notice the contrast between the headings and this paragraph. The heading helps you find the thought; the serif body lets you settle into it. Neither needs to announce itself twice.

### Give the thought some texture

A word can be **confident**, _thoughtful_, or _**both at once**_. A correction can remain visible as ~~a perfect first attempt~~ a useful second draft. A short `inline example` changes the texture again.

#### Look at the small details

Curly quotes “hold a phrase,” while an apostrophe gives a writer’s sentence a little bend. An ellipsis… lets the thought trail away. Parentheses (used sparingly) make space for an aside.

##### A quiet label

Fifth-level headings are here as a specimen. Most articles will be clearer with fewer levels, but a theme should still give the less common ones a sensible place.

###### The smallest heading

This is the sixth and final level. The page title supplies the first level; the article body starts at the second.

## Numbers have a voice, too

0123456789. A notebook can hold 12 ideas, 3 sketches, and 1 useful question. Punctuation changes the rhythm: a colon introduces; a semicolon connects. An ampersand brings ink & paper together.

> Read the sentence aloud. If you run out of breath, the reader might, too.

## Leave a little silence

Short sentences help.

So do longer ones, especially when they follow a clear line of thought and give the reader enough landmarks to reach the end without having to start again. Alternating the two creates a page that feels less like a wall and more like a conversation.
